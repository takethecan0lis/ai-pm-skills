# Decision digest

## Purpose
A status report that lists news and leaves the questions for the end gets skimmed, and the decisions in it stall. This skill turns the report into a page built around answers. The owner sees wins, then what needs them, with enough context on each question to reply without opening anything else, and replies once.

## Objective and benefit
**Objective:** Each digest lets the owner see what happened, understand each decision, and answer all of them in one sitting on a phone.

**Benefit:** It aims to make decisions easier by giving every question its context, options, recommendation and the cost of waiting. It aims to cut reading by removing stale lines and asking nothing twice. A default and a deadline are meant to keep work moving when the owner is away. Without it, questions arrive without the facts needed to answer them, the owner has to hunt through other files, replies slip, and work waits on a decision nobody realised was theirs.

**Why it is worth having:** It aims at decisions made on time, from a page the owner trusts to be current. The outcome it is built for is less reading, fewer follow-up questions and fewer decisions that wait unseen.

## When to use
- You report periodically to someone who owns decisions about your work, and some of those decisions are open.
- Your updates run long, the owner skims them, and questions get missed or answered late.
- The owner reads on a phone and cannot open other documents to find the background.
- Skip it for a plain factual answer or a note with nothing to decide. A short message is better there.

## Steps
1. Before writing, read the previous digest and the answers the owner gave to it. Open the new digest with one short passage on what changed since those answers: what was acted on, what was decided by default, what is still open. If the previous digest is still unanswered, do not publish a second one. Update that page instead, so only one unanswered digest exists at a time.
2. Open with the wins, ahead of everything else, each stated plainly. Use real counts only when you can say where they came from. If a figure cannot be computed or traced, say that in the line rather than leaving it out or showing it as healthy.
3. Group the rest by project or area. In each area write three short parts in this order: what worked, what changed, and what needs the owner. Keep the first two brief, because the third is the reason the page exists.
4. Allow at most three asks per area. Rank them by how much each blocks progress and keep the top ones. Anything you can settle yourself and reverse later, and that installs nothing, exposes nothing to a network and writes nothing outside the work, is not an ask: take the recommended answer, and list it in a "decided by default" line the owner can undo by naming it.
5. Write each ask so the decision comes first, in plain words, with any item number or label placed after it and explained beside it. Give the recommended answer straight after the decision, not a menu. State the deadline if one exists, and say what happens if the owner stays silent, such as which default is taken on which date.
6. Give each ask its full context directly under it: what happened, why it matters, the realistic options and what each would cause, your recommendation and why, and the risk of waiting. Write it so the owner could answer from the page alone. If a question needs another file to be understood, put the needed facts into the question and drop the link.
7. Mark reserved decisions clearly. A reserved decision is one only the owner may take: money, accounts and credentials, anything sent or posted on the owner's behalf, deletions. A reserved ask never gets a default, and the page says "reserved" next to it. Where one decision must follow another, say "after item N" so the owner answers in the right order.
8. Put a visible marker at the point where the owner can stop reading, so that everything above it is enough to act on. Place detail, history and supporting numbers below it.
9. Ask each thing once. If a question appears in an area card, it does not reappear in a summary, a list of waiting items or a closing note.
10. Remove stale content before publishing. Give every status line an as-of date, and delete or refresh any line you cannot date. A line you cannot vouch for should not be on the page.
11. Add a section headed "what this did not do or verify" before the closing line. List the areas not checked and the reason, and name anything reported as built or designed that has not been run. Never leave it empty.
12. Give each question its own answer box and each section a comment box, with a final box for anything else. Put no answer box under text that holds no question. End the page with a single button the owner presses when done, so the digest records when the owner is done.
13. Store the finished digest and, when they arrive, the owner's answers and comments together in one place you can find again. The next digest reads them first, which is what step 1 depends on. A comment that would change a rule, a control or an outside system is confirmed with the owner before anyone acts on it.

## Example
Invented data. Product team "Pebble Notes", digest dated 2026-03-09. Six lines of a sample outline.

1. Wins (as of 2026-03-09): onboarding checklist live for all new accounts since last Thursday. The count of finished signups comes from the signup log, which was readable.
2. Area card, Mobile app. Worked: crash reports fell after the last release. Changed: the offline mode release moved by one week. Needs you: one ask, below.
3. Ask 1. Ship offline mode to all users on 2026-03-16, or to a 10 percent group first? Recommended: the 10 percent group first. Context: offline mode lets people edit without a signal, and it changes how notes sync. It passed the team's tests, but real sync conflicts only show up with real users. Options: all users (everyone gets it at once, a bad conflict affects every note) or a 10 percent group (a bad conflict stays small, full release slips about a week). Risk of waiting: the competitor's offline release lands in April. Deadline: 2026-03-13. If you stay silent: the 10 percent group goes out on 2026-03-16.
4. Your answer box, followed by the area comment box.
5. You can stop reading here. Below this line is history and detail.
6. What this did not do or verify: sync behavior on tablets was not tested; the finished-signup count excludes accounts created before 2026-01-01 (as of 2026-03-09).

## What this does not verify
- It does not make the decisions better. It makes them easier to see and answer. A weak recommendation is still weak on a clear page.
- A long digest still gets skimmed. The cap on asks and the stop marker help, but they do not force reading.
- Owners may answer without reading the context. A silent owner triggers a default, and that default can be wrong.
- Stale facts mislead. The as-of dates only help if the author keeps them honest.
- Adopted, not measured: no result is claimed for faster answers, fewer follow-up questions or fewer missed decisions.

## Common failure modes
- Asks buried below the status text, so the owner finishes reading before reaching a question.
- Questions that need another file to answer, which a phone reader cannot open.
- Too many asks in one area, so the important one is lost among the minor ones.
- No default and no deadline, so a silent owner stalls the work.
- Counts nobody can trace, with no source named and no word on what could not be computed.
- Stale lines left in place, carrying no as-of date and still read as current.
- One digest piling onto another while the first is still unanswered, so the owner faces two pages of open questions.
- The same question asked in two places, which invites two different answers.
